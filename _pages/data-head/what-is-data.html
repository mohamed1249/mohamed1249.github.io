  • Data is encoded information. That’s a very clean mental model. Information exists first; data is how we represent, store, transmit, and analyze it. And because encoding is never perfectly neutral, what gets measured, how it gets measured, and what gets left out matter.
  • Avoid vague words like “usual,” “typical,” or “normal.” Because those words often hide an undefined statistical claim. “Typical” could mean mean, median, mode, majority, expected range, or just someone’s intuition.
  • Know your audience: “feature,” “variable,” and “predictor” can describe similar things, but different fields speak different dialects. Good data people translate.
  • Observational vs. experimental data: observing what happens is different from deliberately changing something and measuring the effect. That distinction matters heavily for causal claims.
  • Randomized A/B tests: randomization tries to balance confounding factors, making comparisons much more credible.
