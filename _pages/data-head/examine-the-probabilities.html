  • This chapter is mainly about learning to reason with probability instead of just reading percentages.
  • The strongest ideas from your screenshots are:
    • Conditional probability matters: P(A | B) asks how likely A is once B is known.
    • Independence is an assumption, not a default. Two events are independent only when knowing one gives you no information about the other.
    • For events happening together:
      • if independent, multiply probabilities;
      • in general, use the conditional version.
    • Don’t swap dependencies: P(A | B) is usually not the same as P(B | A). This is one of the most common probability mistakes.
    • All probabilities are conditional in some sense. They depend on assumptions, context, population, measurement quality, and circumstances.
    • Calibration gives probabilities meaning: if something is predicted at 75%, it should happen roughly 75% of the time across many similar predictions.
    • People can also game probabilities through selection bias—for example, only taking easy cases and then advertising a high success rate.
  • Rare events can, and do, happen. Low probability does not mean impossible. If enough opportunities exist, even very unlikely outcomes eventually appear.
  • The virus-test example is also excellent because it shows why base rates matter: even a very accurate test can produce a surprisingly large number of false positives when the thing being tested for is rare.
  • I’d compress the chapter into: A probability without its conditions, assumptions, base rate, and calibration can be dangerously misleading.
